Which search pages should an editor open first?

Muhammad Saad Imran · August 2026 · Lane 4, click-through opportunity scoring · notebooks and code

Abstract

FlyRank publishes content into client websites and then watches search data to keep it working, flagging pages that need attention with hand-written threshold rules — but an editor can review only about fifty pages a week from a portfolio of hundreds of thousands, so the decision worth supporting is not which pages are underperforming (thousands are) but which one to open first. Using three months of Google Search Console data from the FlyRank internship warehouse, I built features from January and February 2026 and defined the outcome from March: a page under-captured clicks if its March click-through rate fell in the bottom quarter of pages sharing its February search-position tier. A gradient-boosted classifier was then compared against a frozen transparent rule on the same rows, the same client-grouped folds, the same metric and the same tie policy. Across five folds in which no client appeared on both sides, the model reached a mean precision@50 of 0.784 against the rule's 0.492 and a base rate of 0.216, winning in every fold — though roughly half that margin rests on click-history features and the fold-to-fold spread runs from 0.60 to 0.90. The output is a ranked review queue with reason codes: decision support for ordering a week of editorial attention, not a claim that editing a page produces clicks.

Introduction

Where this problem comes from

FlyRank builds content as infrastructure. It researches, writes and publishes content directly into a client's website, then watches search data and optimises — the full cycle, run by algorithms rather than by people doing it by hand. Content that gets found in search ranks, and then quietly decays: rankings slip, clicks drop, and most teams notice too late.

The product already responds to this. It flags pages today with a health score, quick-win tags and needs-attention markers. Those flags are hand-written rules — if-this-then-that, with thresholds chosen by a person. They work, and they run in production. But rules run out where signals get many, tangled and shifting, and at the time of writing no trained model has replaced them.

That gap is the whole of this paper. I took one of those rules, froze it as a baseline, and asked whether a learned model beats it on identical terms. Because the product's flags are the output of a decision someone already made, they are used here strictly as something to beat — never as model inputs. A model trained on an existing system's scores learns the old rule, not the world.

The decision, concretely

An editor sits down on Monday with a list of pages and about fifty reviews' worth of time. The portfolio runs to hundreds of thousands of pages. Asking "which pages are underperforming?" returns a list far longer than the week, so the question does no work. The question that does work is narrower and harder: of everything I could open, which one first?

That is a ranking problem, and it has a specific shape. The cost of a wrong call is not a wrong answer — it is an hour spent on a page that was fine, while a page that needed attention sat in the queue. So the metric has to measure the top of the list, not the average of it.

I chose the click-through opportunity lane: find pages that are shown in search but under-capture clicks relative to comparable pages. A page ranking in position eight that converts well is not the problem. A page in position eight that converts far below other position-eight pages is losing clicks that already exist — the impressions are there, the click is not.

The obvious rule, and why it failed

My first approach was a transparent rule with no model in it. Every page sits in a search-position tier; score each page by how far its click-through rate falls below the typical rate for its tier, and rank by that gap. It is explainable, cheap, and requires no training.

Before building anything on top of it, I tested whether the assumption held. It did not. The tier rule was only 5.6% better than ignoring position entirely and predicting one flat number for every page. Worse, its top 100 pages all received the same score — one distinct value across the entire top of the queue — so the rule could not order the one part of the list that actually gets used.

That negative result set up everything after it. The rule became a frozen baseline — something a model has to beat on identical terms — rather than the product.

Data

What was excluded, and why

203,074 pages appeared in at least one of the three months. The study population is 40,152 pages across 24 clients, after four filters:

  • Present in all three months. A page needs January and February to have features and March to have an outcome.
  • At least 500 February impressions. A page nobody sees is not an editorial opportunity, and a click-through rate on twelve impressions is noise.
  • At least 100 March impressions. March click-through rate is a rate; on three impressions it carries no information.
  • Real February position data. In this warehouse an average position of zero means no data, not rank zero — a distinction that would silently corrupt the tier assignment if treated as a number.

Two field families were excluded deliberately rather than by filtering. trend_direction and trend_pct are derived from the outcome and would leak it. Hashed page and client identifiers are used for grouping during validation and are never features — an identifier as a feature is a memorisation channel, not a signal.

Missing values were kept missing. The model handles them natively, and replacing a missing value with zero would assert that nothing happened when in fact nothing was recorded.

Methodology

Assumptions, stated so they can be disagreed with

  • Comparable pages are pages in the same February search-position tier. Comparing a top-three page's click-through rate against a deep page's would only rediscover position.
  • An editor's constraint is attention, roughly fifty reviews a week, so the useful output is an ordered queue rather than a classification of every page.
  • A page's own recent history is legitimate evidence about its near future.

Label definition

A page under-captured clicks if its March click-through rate fell below the 25th percentile of March click-through rate for its February position tier. The percentile cut is computed inside each training fold and applied to the held-out fold — never over the whole dataset. This is a proxy for editorial value, and I name it as one: it measures under-capture relative to peers, not revenue.

Features

Eleven numeric fields and two categorical, all knowable by 28 February: January and February impressions and clicks, January and February click-through rate, February average position, a January-to-February impressions ratio, the count of February days the page appeared in search, word count, content type and main intent — plus one injected pure-noise column that serves as a significance floor for the importance analysis.

Baseline

The frozen Week-4 rule described in the introduction: expected click-through rate is the median for the page's February tier; the score is the shortfall multiplied by February impressions. Its tier medians are refitted inside every training fold. Computing them once over the whole panel would let the rule see held-out clients' data before being tested on them — a subtle leak that would flatter the baseline, not the model.

Validation design

Five-fold GroupKFold grouped on the hashed client identifier, so a client's pages are never on both sides of a split. Zero client overlap is asserted in code rather than assumed. The model's internal early stopping is switched off: it carves out a validation set at random with no knowledge of client, which would leak clients inside every fold while I was carefully holding them out on the outside.

Model and baseline sit the same exam — same rows, same folds, same metric and K, same tie policy. Ties matter here more than they sound, because the baseline's known failure is producing identical scores; the tie-break is written down (score, then February impressions, then page id) and used identically by every competitor.

Leakage checks

Three, run explicitly rather than assumed:

  1. Timeline audit. Every feature traced to its source window. All sit before March. This is what clears the click-history features: January and February clicks are knowable at decision time. The rule is a temporal boundary, not a ban on a data type.
  2. Harness test. March click-through rate — the column the label is computed from — was deliberately injected as a feature. Precision@50 went from 0.784 to a clean 1.000. A validation setup that cannot produce that jump cannot detect leakage, and every other number here would be unfalsifiable.
  3. Population audit. The eligibility filters were checked for outcome-window information. They contain some, and Section 5 states exactly how much.

The model beats the rule in every fold, not on average. Per fold the model scored 0.90 / 0.78 / 0.84 / 0.80 / 0.60 against the rule's 0.50 / 0.66 / 0.38 / 0.50 / 0.42. That matters because a mean can hide one lucky fold; here it does not.

Complexity was not free money. A depth-3 decision tree — a model that can be printed and read in full — already reaches 0.692, and a random forest reaches 0.760 with the lowest fold-to-fold spread of any method. Most of the available lift comes from using the features at all, not from the boosting on top. If asked to defend one model in a room, I would defend the forest.

How much of this is the honest split?

I described the client-level holdout as a strength for several weeks without ever measuring what it was worth. Re-running the identical model under a plain random split — clients allowed on both sides — gives 0.836 against the grouped 0.784, an apparent cost of 0.052.

That reading is wrong, and finding out why changed the conclusion. The base rate also moves between the two splits, from 0.216 to 0.246, because the label cut is fitted on the training fold and transfers imperfectly to unseen clients. 0.030 of the 0.052 — 57% of it — is the target moving, not skill lost. Measured as lift over its own base rate the gap is 0.022; measured as a multiple of base rate the grouped model is slightly ahead, 3.63× against 3.40×.

Effect size, not just direction

Across the five paired folds, Cohen's d for model against rule is 2.04 — large by any convention. It is computed on five folds, three of which hold a single test client, so it is an order of magnitude rather than a measurement. Notably the grouped split has the larger mean advantage (+0.292 against +0.276) but the smaller effect size, because the folds disagree more once clients are separated. Magnitude and reliability are different questions.

What the model leans on

Permutation importance on held-out data, with the injected noise column as the significance floor and the documentation's own rule (mean − 2·sd > 0):

  • Roughly half the advantage is borrowed from click history. Removing the four click-derived features drops the model from 0.784 to 0.604 — still ahead of the rule, by a 0.112 margin instead of 0.292. The depth-3 tree collapses further, 0.692 to 0.364.
  • Two features are dead weight. Content type scores exactly 0.0000 — the model never splits on it. January impressions fail the significance test (0.0040 ± 0.0033) and sit barely above the injected noise, which itself ranked 12th of 13.
  • Four feature pairs exceed |r| = 0.70, with January and February clicks at 0.95, so those importances are depressed for both members of each pair and should be read as a floor.

Where it is wrong

The errors have one recognisable shape. In the worst fold — precision 0.600 against a best of 0.900 — every inspected miss had a February click-through rate of exactly 0.000% and a March rate that recovered on its own to between 0.090% and 0.387%. The model learned that a page converting nothing is a strong signal; for these pages February was simply a bad month. A two-month feature window cannot separate "this page under-converts" from "this page had one quiet month", and no amount of tuning changes that.

A belief of mine was also reversed. In week four I named near-zero-click pages as the picks I trusted least. The model does the opposite: it puts more of them at the top and is right far more often than the rule on them. Across the full panel, pages with real impressions and no February clicks under-captured 47.4% of the time against 17.8% for everything else. My caution was not supported, and recording that is more useful than quietly dropping it.

Limitations and honest framing

Scope. This queue ranks pages that were still receiving search impressions during the outcome month. A page that vanished from search entirely cannot be scored at all — March click-through rate is a rate, and a rate needs a denominator. Measured: 979 of 41,234 February-eligible pages (2.4%) produced no March row at all, with a median of 864 February impressions, so they were genuinely visible before they went. They are structurally absent from this work and are arguably the pages most in need of attention.

The eligibility filter reads the outcome month, and I did not disclose that until late. Requiring 100 March impressions removes 102 pages (0.3%); removing that filter moves the score up to 0.828, so it was never inflating anything. The requirement that a page appear in all three months is the one that matters, and it is not patchable — it is a property of choosing a rate as the label.

Roughly half the signal is click history, measured once. The model still clears the rule without it, but by a much narrower margin, and that is the number I would quote to someone deciding whether to build on this.

Five folds, three holding a single test client. The spread runs 0.60 to 0.90. Treat the headline as directional.

Nothing here is causal. The model observed which pages under-captured clicks. It says nothing about whether editing them produces clicks — that needs a controlled comparison nobody has run. The output is decision support for ordering attention, and no more.

One negative result, reported because it is a result. I expected declining pages to be the priority — the familiar content-decay story. Across the panel every month-on-month momentum bucket sits between 0.198 and 0.242 against a 0.225 base rate: momentum barely separates pages in either direction. Below-tier conversion is what separates them. Conditioned on already converting below tier, slipping pages do carry signal, so both views have to be read together — but decline on its own is not the priority signal it is usually assumed to be.

The sealed month is untouched. June 2026 has never been queried, across every notebook in this project. No claim in this paper rests on it.

Ranked recommendations

The model's output is a queue. Its most useful property is not the ordering of the top — it is what it sets aside. Half the portfolio (20,061 of 40,152 pages) falls into a “watch only” group whose under-capture rate is 0.083 against a 0.225 base rate. An editor who ignores that half is ignoring pages that under-capture less than a third as often as average. Not spending the week in the wrong half is worth more than the ordering of the right half.

Rules for the person using it

Every reason code attached to a page is a fact that can be checked by opening the page. They exist so an editor can overrule the queue — if a code contradicts what they know, that is a signal to investigate, and overruling is a correct use.

The queue is not a safe list. It says which pages to review first. It never says a page is fine. Absence from it means a low score, no score, or outside the population — and those are not the same thing. Acting on absence is the one misuse that would do real harm.

Do not automate the actions, and do not retrain on a drift alert — data-quality bugs look exactly like drift, and retraining on unchecked data bakes the bug in.

The largest operational risk is concentration, not accuracy. The top fifty draws from 8 of 24 clients, and a single client holds 44% of it. The pages are genuine; nobody should be handed that week without being told.

Monitoring, given that the truth arrives late

Whether a page under-captured in March is only knowable in April. Model quality cannot be watched directly, so monitoring is proxy: watch the inputs and the outputs for signs the world changed.

Choosing what to watch had to be measured rather than assumed. My first trigger was "twice the largest normal month-to-month move" — but page impressions shift with a population stability index of 1.346 between two ordinary months, five times past the conventional 0.25 "major shift" line. That rule produced an alarm that could never fire. A feature whose normal month is already a major shift cannot detect drift at all. Impressions are now a data-quality check; click-through rate, stable at 0.031, is the drift sentinel.

One reproducibility defect worth recording. An early version assigned the noise column by row position, so rebuilding the data pull in a different order silently moved every number. Fixing the row order by page id before generating noise made consecutive runs byte-identical. It was found by re-running, not by reading the code — which is the argument for re-running.
